# A Judge's Book of Wonders: Reading al-Qazwini's Wonders of Creation

In 1280, in the Iraqi city of Wasit, a copyist finished a manuscript that tried to describe the whole of creation, from the outermost sphere of the heavens down to stones, birds and the jinn. That copy now sits in the Bavarian State Library in Munich as Cod.arab. 464. It is the oldest known witness to Zakariyya al-Qazwini's *ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt wa gharāʾib al-mawjūdāt*, usually shortened in English to *The Wonders of Creation*, and it was completed three years before its author died [2]. Its hundreds of paintings, full of giant birds, sea monsters and beings labelled as jinn, are how most people meet the book today.

A recent video essay from the channel Where Faith Leads walks through that book in about 36 minutes [1]. Its framing question is a good one: why would a working judge, rather than a teller of tales, fill a cosmography with talking trees and dog-headed men? This post follows the video's route and adds the scholarship behind it, so a curious reader can check each claim and keep going.

## Who al-Qazwini was

Abu Yahya Zakariyya ibn Muhammad al-Qazwini was born around 1203 in Qazvin, in today's Iran, and died in 1283 [3]. The video places him in a family of jurists and describes his flight from Qazvin as a teenager, when the first Mongol campaigns reached the region around 1220. He continued his studies in Mosul and later served as a qadi, a judge, in the Iraqi cities of Wasit and Hilla [1].

He wrote his major works in Arabic, although *Encyclopaedia Iranica* notes small infelicities of style that suggest Arabic was not his first language [3]. Calling him an Arab, Persian or Islamic scholar therefore emphasises different parts of one life that crossed those boundaries. His patron was ʿAta-Malik Juvayni, the historian who governed Iraq and Khuzestan for the first Mongol Ilkhans, and the cosmography is dedicated to him [3]. Al-Qazwini also wrote a companion geography, *Āthār al-bilād*, which matters later in this story.

## Wonder as a way of looking

The book does not open with monsters. It opens with four prologues: one on what a marvel is and why it holds the mind, one on the kinds of created things, one defining the strange and remarkable, and one on how existing things are divided [3]. In the video's paraphrase, a marvel is something that bewilders you because you cannot see its cause, and the strange is whatever departs from the familiar [1].

That definition is the key to the whole project. Travis Zadeh's study of the book argues that contemplating the rare and astonishing was understood as a pious discipline, a way of reading creation as evidence of design [4]. Persis Berlekamp makes a parallel case about the illustrated copies: their images were meant to induce wonder at God's creation, which was the book's stated purpose [5]. The video draws out one of al-Qazwini's own arguments in that spirit. If creation already contains creatures that seem impossible, it is hasty to dismiss beings we cannot see, such as angels [1].

## How the cosmos is ordered

After the prologues come two discourses [3]. The first covers everything above the sphere of the moon: the moon, sun, planets and fixed stars, with explanations of eclipses along the way. The model is the earth-centred cosmos of nested spheres inherited from Ptolemaic astronomy. The same discourse turns to the inhabitants of the heavens, including the bearers of the divine throne, Gabriel and the other archangels, guardian angels, and Munkar and Nakir, who question the dead. It closes with time itself, comparing the calendars of the Arabs, the Syrians and the Persians.

The second discourse is about four times as long. It begins with the four elements, winds, rainbows, thunder and lightning, then divides the earth into seven climes and tours its seas and islands. Finally it works through minerals, plants and animals, beginning with the human being and moving on to the jinn and then to beasts, birds and creeping things [3]. The video points out that some of the weather explanations come surprisingly close to modern ones, even when they are framed in the elemental theory of the time [1].

One caution belongs here. *Iranica* reports that the Arabic text circulated in at least four recensions, and that Ferdinand Wüstenfeld's pioneering 1848–49 edition used the latest of them while adding material from other manuscripts [3][11]. "What al-Qazwini wrote" therefore depends on which copy you are reading. The 1280 Munich manuscript is valuable because it sits so close to the author's own lifetime [2].

## The marvels everyone remembers

The islands are where the book earns its reputation. Al-Qazwini describes island peoples who move between trees, others with wings, and the island of Ramni, with camphor trees large enough to shade a hundred people [1]. The Waqwaq islands carry the "talking tree" of the video's title: a tree whose fruit resembles human beings and calls out as it falls, giving the islands their name [1].

Dragon Island is the most cinematic episode. In al-Qazwini's telling, a dragon devouring the islanders' livestock is killed when Alexander, known as Iskandar, feeds it oxen stuffed with sulphur and iron hooks. The grateful islanders give him a yellow hare with a single black horn, and every wild animal flees at the sight of it [1]. The Munich manuscript illustrates the scene, which shows how closely the painters followed the text [1][2]. Among the birds, the ʿAnqāʾ, the Simurgh of Persian tradition, lives alone on Mount Qaf, ancient and wise [1].

Gog and Magog, Yaʾjuj and Maʾjuj in Arabic, appear most fully in the companion geography [1]. Their story begins in the Qur'an, where Dhul-Qarnayn builds a barrier against them that will hold until an appointed time [6][7]. Later tradition elaborated it, and in the ninth century the caliph al-Wathiq reportedly sent an envoy, Sallam the Interpreter, to find the wall [8]. The video adds an interpretation worth weighing: al-Qazwini lived through the Mongol conquests, so a legend about an unstoppable force from the east may have felt less like distant history to his readers [1]. That reading is plausible, but it is the presenter's argument rather than something the text states.

## Images travel faster than captions

The video's opening example is its sharpest lesson. A Persian copy held by the United States National Library of Medicine shows a crowned, winged creature with a human face that looks like a portrait of a spirit. The folio actually belongs to the section on the heavens, beside Pisces and Orion [1]. NYU's Institute for the Study of the Ancient World displayed that opening from the library's MS P 1, copied in western India in 1537–38. The figure is the constellation Cetus [9]. The auto-generated captions mangle the name, which is a small reminder to check names against a catalogue.

The occult material is not imagined, though. A later NLM copy, probably made in eighteenth-century western India, includes magic squares and illustrates a chapter on the subjugation of jinn [10]. One of al-Qazwini's Persian sources, *Tuḥfat al-gharāʾib*, which he quotes more than thirty times, mixes natural wonders with tricks and stratagems [3]. The honest summary is that the tradition contains several kinds of knowledge at once, and individual copies added or emphasised different parts over centuries.

## What the video gets right, and where to read next

The video's structure matches the scholarly descriptions of the book closely, and it is careful to flag weak sources. One example is a report about the sun's speed that it says is not established as an authentic hadith [1]. It ends with an admission that some chapters can be checked against the Qur'an and authentic hadith while others are simply old stories. That is a sensible position for a popular presentation. The video includes a sponsor segment, and its interpretive claims, such as the Mongol reading of Gog and Magog, are best treated as prompts for further reading.

For that reading, Zadeh's *Wonders and Rarities* follows the book's long journey through languages, courts and collectors [4]. Berlekamp's *Wonder, Image, and Cosmos in Medieval Islam* explains how the paintings were meant to work [5]. The digitised Munich manuscript lets you see the 1280 copy for yourself [2]. Read together, they show a judge's book of wonders as a method rather than a curiosity cabinet: look closely, admit what you cannot explain, and let astonishment lead you back to the order behind things.
